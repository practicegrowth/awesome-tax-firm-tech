# Measuring local and search visibility for tax and accounting firms

This worksheet is a vendor-neutral starting point for a tax, CPA, accounting, bookkeeping, or tax-resolution firm measuring how its public information is found on Google Search. It is not a promise of rankings, traffic, calls, reviews, leads, or revenue. It does not replace a firm’s own privacy, advertising, ethics, or client-communication review.

## 1. Set up an evidence baseline

Before changing a website, profile, or review workflow, record the date range, the property or profile reviewed, the owner, and the exact data source. Keep reports separate when their metrics measure different things.

| Question | Evidence to retain |
| --- | --- |
| Is the website verified in Search Console? | Property type, verified owner, and access roles. |
| Which pages are eligible to be found? | Sitemap status, index-status findings, and URL Inspection results where relevant. |
| Which search queries and pages are receiving visibility? | Date range and Search Console Performance report export or notes. |
| What did a visitor do after arriving? | Separate analytics evidence, if the firm uses it, with the metric definition recorded. |
| Which firm information can a prospective client see? | A dated review of public contact, service, location, and intake information. |

Google describes Search Console as a way to understand how a site is crawled, indexed, and served in Google Search. Its Performance report can show clicks, impressions, and related breakdowns by queries and pages. Confirm current report availability and definitions in the product. [Google Search Console documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/monitor-debug/search-console-start)

## 2. Use a simple monthly review

Compare a stable date range with an equivalent earlier range and write down meaningful website or business changes beside the data. Do not treat one metric change as proof of a marketing outcome.

| Review area | Questions to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Search visibility | Which queries and pages changed in impressions, clicks, click-through rate, or average position? |
| Technical discoverability | Are there new indexing, crawl, sitemap, structured-data, security, or manual-action messages to investigate? |
| Content and client intent | Do the pages receiving attention match the services and questions the firm can actually support? |
| Public business information | Are contact details, service descriptions, hours, and intake routes accurate and consistent with firm operations? |
| Review operations | Does the firm have a documented, permitted process for requesting, monitoring, and responding to feedback? |
| Conversion evidence | If the firm measures enquiries or appointments, are definitions, consent requirements, and attribution limits documented separately? |

## 3. Record the change, owner, and next check

For every meaningful change, record the page or profile, purpose, source evidence, owner, date, and the next review date. Examples include correcting inaccurate public information, resolving an indexing error, or updating a service page after a firm-approved change.

Avoid claims that a change will improve rankings or generate a stated number of leads. Treat Search Console data as Google Search visibility evidence and site-analytics data as on-site behavior evidence; Google notes that similar metrics across the tools need not match exactly. [Google’s explanation of Search Console and Analytics data](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/monitor-debug/google-analytics-search-console)

## 4. Escalate instead of guessing

Pause and obtain the appropriate review when a proposed change involves professional-service claims, testimonials or incentives, client information, location representation, third-party platform rules, or regulated advertising and privacy questions.
